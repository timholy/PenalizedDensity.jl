PenalizedDensity.AbstractDensityEstimate — Type
AbstractDensityEstimate

Supertype of the fitted densities: DensityEstimate (point observations) and IntervalDensityEstimate (interval observations, optionally mixed with points). Every subtype supports:

  • evaluation: d(x) (the density), amplitude, logdensity, logdensity_eval_gradient, cdf and quantile. Evaluating any of these at NaN throws a DomainError;
  • the support queries minimum, maximum and extrema;
  • sampling by inversion through rand: rand(d), rand(rng, d, dims...) and rand!(rng, A, d) draw from the fitted density;
  • the fit's moments mean, var and std, its median, and the observation count nobs;
  • entropy, negentropy (in-sample and held-out) and action;
  • the probit transform gaussianize, its inverse ungaussianize, and gaussianize_logjacobian.

The pointwise functions take one point at a time, and a fit acts as a scalar in broadcasting: cdf.(d, xs) evaluates the CDF at each element of xs.

The goodness-of-fit statistic (chisq, ChisqReference) and logdensity_node_gradient are defined for DensityEstimate only.

Fits also work with DensityInterface.jl, Distributions.jl and StatsBase.jl (see the extended help). Loading Distributions.jl makes the unqualified names cdf and entropy ambiguous, and loading StatsBase.jl does the same for entropy; either qualified name gives the same result.

Extended help

Every subtype has the fields d.x (the sorted, distinct nodes), d.ψ (the normalized amplitude at the nodes) and d.κ (the smoothing scale: one number, or a vector with one rate per cell between consecutive nodes). Other fields are specific to each subtype.

The node cumulatives the mass functions need are assembled when the fit is constructed, so evaluating a pointwise function costs a binary search over the nodes plus a constant amount of work.

Interoperability

Every subtype implements DensityInterface.jl as an object that has a density (DensityKind(d) == HasDensity(), as for a Distributions.jl distribution): logdensityof(d, x) == logdensity(d, x) and densityof(d, x) == d(x) at a real x.

When Distributions.jl is loaded, Distributions.cdf, Distributions.pdf and Distributions.logpdf accept a fit and agree with cdf, d(x) and logdensity; quantile is already shared with Distributions through Statistics. When StatsBase.jl is loaded, StatsBase.entropy(d) agrees with entropy(d). The held-out entropy(d, xeval) has no StatsBase counterpart, because StatsBase's two-argument entropy(p, b) takes a logarithm base.

PenalizedDensity.Adaptive — Type
Adaptive(; alphas=(0.125, 0.25, …, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5), window=WindowRule(),
         nse=(window === nothing ? 1 : 0), pilot=nothing)

The search settings of select_kappa(x, Adaptive(; …)), which chooses a spatially varying smoothing scale.

  • alphas: the exponents of the power family c·(p̂/ḡ)^α. They must be positive, because α = 0 is the constant scale, which is always in the comparison. They are stored, and searched, in increasing order. The default is (0.125, 0.25, 0.375, 0.5, 0.625, 0.75, 0.875, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5).
  • window: the window rule's settings (a WindowRule), or nothing to leave out the window candidate.
  • nse ≥ 0: how many standard errors the power family's gain over the constant scale must exceed. nse = 0 returns whichever has the smaller score. The default is 0 with a window candidate and 1 without, so that the power family alone must beat the constant scale clearly.
  • pilot: a callable returning the positive constant scale of the pilot density from the sample, or from (lower, upper) for interval data. The default nothing uses the constant-scale select_kappa.

PenalizedDensity.AdaptiveScale — Type
AdaptiveScale(c, α, pilot)

A spatially varying smoothing scale of the plug-in form

κ(x) = c · (p̂(x) / ḡ)^α,

where p̂ is the pilot density estimate and ḡ the geometric mean of p̂ over the sample it was fitted to. select_kappa(x, Adaptive()) constructs one by choosing c and α. An AdaptiveScale is callable and is passed to DensityEstimate as the smoothing scale.

Dividing by ḡ puts c on the same footing as a constant scale: where p̂ equals its geometric mean, κ(x) = c. The exponent α > 0 sets how strongly the scale follows the density: larger α resolves the peaks more finely and smooths the tails more heavily.

Extended help

The scale is floored at 1e-6 c to prevent underflow of (p̂/ḡ)^α where the pilot density is negligible; the floor is far below any scale the rule would otherwise choose.

PenalizedDensity.ChisqReference — Type
ChisqReference(d::DensityEstimate)

Reference distribution of the goodness-of-fit statistic chisq for the fit d, following Holy 1997 (Eqs. 16–18). The statistic is a quadratic form in the Gaussian fluctuation field of the Laplace approximation about the fit, so its law is a generalized chi-squared; this law is exact for that field at finite N. Construction costs O(N).

Build one per fit and reuse it across evaluations of pvalue, chisq_ccdf and chisq_pdf, for any number of trial densities tested against the fit. These take method = :exact (the finite-N law, the default) or method = :largeN (its large-N limit). The exact mean is mean(ref). A spatially varying κ and a finite support (see DensityEstimate) are both supported, and the cost stays O(N).

It is not, by itself, a finite-sample frequentist null distribution for data drawn from the fitted density; it is a Bayesian measure of likelihood among distributions sharing the same roughness penalty. Its calibration as a frequentist null distribution need not be distribution-free.

Defined for point data only; there is no form for an IntervalDensityEstimate.

Extended help

The object stores O(N) data (a symmetric tridiagonal matrix and a rank-one constraint vector) from which its density and tail probabilities are computed.

With a spatially varying κ the nodal precision of the fluctuation field is 2λ times the same tridiagonal operator the fit assembles, whatever the scale. With a finite support the fluctuation field's natural (Neumann) boundary condition makes M̂ the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of the boundary segments as well as the interior, and the same identity G₀⁻¹ = 2λM̂ holds with M̂ the bounded operator the fit already assembles.

source
PenalizedDensity.ConvergenceError — Type
ConvergenceError(routine, msg)

Thrown when an iterative routine (a fit's Newton solve, a quantile or probit inversion, or a selector's search) stops without converging. routine names it and msg describes the state at which it stopped. The result such a routine would return is unreliable, so it is not returned.

PenalizedDensity.DensityEstimate — Type
DensityEstimate(x, κ; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(float(eltype(x)))))
DensityEstimate{T}(x, κ; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(T)))

Estimate a continuous one-dimensional probability density from sample points x, using the scalar-field method of Holy, Phys. Rev. Lett. 79, 3545 (1997).

κ is either a positive number, giving one scale everywhere, or a callable κ(x) returning the scale local to x; larger κ gives a rougher estimate. See select_kappa for choosing it automatically.

The fit's floating-point type T is the promotion of the data, κ, rtol and any finite support bound; an infinite bound does not widen it. DensityEstimate{T}(x, κ; …) sets T instead, converting the data and parameters to it: DensityEstimate{Float32}(x, κ) fits Float64 data in single precision.

support = (a, b) fits the density on a finite domain instead of all of ℝ; either end may be -Inf/Inf for a one-sided or fully unbounded fit (the default). The estimate Q̂ is exactly zero outside [a, b], cdf reaches exactly 0 at a and 1 at b, and every data point must lie in [a, b] (checked at fit time; a violation, or a ≥ b, throws a DomainError). Data points must be finite even when the support is infinite: a NaN or ±Inf in x, or a value that overflows T, throws an ArgumentError.

Repeated points, and points closer than rtol / κ(x) (i.e. within a fraction rtol of the local smoothing length), are merged into one node carrying the count as its integer weight.

The returned object is callable: d(x) evaluates the estimated density Q̂(x) at any real x and can be broadcast over arrays. Use amplitude for ψ(x).

Examples

julia> d = DensityEstimate([-1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0], 1.0);

julia> d(0.0) > d(2.0)   # denser near the data
true

julia> round(chisq(d, d); digits=8)   # a distribution fits itself perfectly
0.0

julia> a = DensityEstimate([-1.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0], x -> 1 + exp(-x^2));  # sharper near 0

julia> a.κ                                # one rate per inter-node interval
2-element Vector{Float64}:
 1.778800783071405
 1.778800783071405

julia> u = DensityEstimate(range(0.05, 0.95; length=50), 10.0; support=(0.0, 1.0));

julia> u(-0.1), u(1.1)              # zero outside the support
(0.0, 0.0)

julia> cdf(u, 0.0), cdf(u, 1.0)     # cdf hits 0 and 1 exactly at the walls
(0.0, 1.0)

Extended help

The estimate is written as Q̂(x) = ψ(x)^2, where the amplitude ψ minimizes the action

S[ψ] = ∫ (λ/κ(x)²) (ψ')² dx - 2 Σᵢ ln ψ(xᵢ)

subject to ∫ ψ² dx = 1, with λ the normalization multiplier. The smoothing scale κ sets the width of each point's contribution, and the penalty weight 1/κ(x)² on (ψ')² keeps the pressure to normalize spatially uniform.

A callable κ(x) is evaluated at the midpoint of each inter-node interval, and at the outermost nodes for the two tails, so the fit resolves a piecewise-constant scale: d.κ[k] is the rate on (d.x[k], d.x[k+1]), and d.κL, d.κR the tail rates. Making κ large where the density is high and small where it is low gives finer resolution where there are enough data to support it.

Between sorted data points ψ solves ψ'' = κ² ψ, i.e. it is a sum of rising and falling exponentials, and decays as e^{-κ|x|} in the tails. The nodal amplitudes ψ(xᵢ) satisfy a symmetric tridiagonal system whose solution is the minimizer of a strictly convex potential; normalization is then a rescaling. Without the merging of close points into one node, this system can be nearly singular.

At a finite support endpoint the density is left free rather than pinned to zero (a natural, or Neumann, boundary condition: ψ'(a) = 0). The wall changes only the outermost interval on that side, replacing its exponential tail with a cosh arc that is flat at the wall, so a discontinuous edge (a "jump edge") is represented directly rather than approximated by a fast-decaying tail.

The goodness-of-fit functions (ChisqReference and everything built on it) support a varying κ and a finite support, in addition to a constant κ on the unbounded line.

Passing a SolveStats as the stats keyword records the Newton-step and backtracking counts of the fixed-scale solve, for benchmarking; it does not change the fit.

PenalizedDensity.IntervalDensityEstimate — Type
IntervalDensityEstimate(lower, upper, κ; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(R)))
IntervalDensityEstimate(x, κ; resolution, support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(R)))
IntervalDensityEstimate{T}(lower, upper, κ; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(T)))
IntervalDensityEstimate{T}(x, κ; resolution, support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(T)))

Estimate a continuous one-dimensional density from interval observations: observation i is known only to lie in [lower[i], upper[i]]. An observation with lower[i] == upper[i] is an exact point, so a sample may mix exact and interval-valued observations; a bound of -Inf or Inf describes a censored value. Intervals may share endpoints but must not otherwise overlap; overlapping intervals throw an ArgumentError, as does an interval that covers the whole support.

The second form is for data recorded on a lattice of spacing resolution (rounded values): each x[i] stands for the interval [x[i] - resolution/2, x[i] + resolution/2], clipped to the support; a value whose interval does not overlap the interior of the support throws a DomainError. Every x[i] must lie on one lattice x₀ + k·resolution (within resolution/1000), or an ArgumentError names the first value that does not.

κ and support have the same meaning as for DensityEstimate: a positive number or a callable κ(x), and a domain (a, b) outside of which the estimate is zero. Every observation must lie within the support, or a DomainError is thrown. Interval endpoints (and points) closer than rtol / κ(x) are merged. In the default rtol, R is the floating-point type of the observations: float of the element type of x, or of the promoted element types of lower and upper. A κ so large that κ times the width of a cell inside an interval exceeds eps(T)^(-1/4) throws an ArgumentError: the fit cannot be computed accurately there, and the estimate has already reached its histogram limit.

The fit's floating-point type T is the promotion of the observations, κ, rtol, resolution and any finite support bound. IntervalDensityEstimate{T}(…), in either form, sets T instead and converts the observations and parameters to it.

The estimate maximizes the penalized likelihood in which an interval observation contributes the log-probability of its interval rather than a log-density, so concentrating density at a point inside an interval does not raise the likelihood. Rounded data are therefore not pulled toward point masses at the recorded values; the finest structure they can support is the histogram at the rounding increment.

The result is callable, d(x) giving the density, and supports every operation listed under AbstractDensityEstimate: evaluation, sampling, moments, entropy and action, and the gaussianize family. The goodness-of-fit statistic chisq and logdensity_node_gradient are not defined for interval data. With point observations only, the fit equals DensityEstimate(x, κ).

Examples

julia> x = round.([-1.23, -0.52, -0.48, 0.07, 0.11, 0.13, 0.66, 1.41]; digits=1);

julia> d = IntervalDensityEstimate(x, 2.0; resolution=0.1);

julia> cdf(d, -Inf), cdf(d, Inf)
(0.0, 1.0)

julia> c = IntervalDensityEstimate([0.0, 0.5, 1.0], [0.0, 0.5, Inf], 1.0);  # 1.0 is right-censored

Extended help

The amplitude ψ = √Q̂ minimizes

S[ψ] = ∫ (λ/κ(x)²) (ψ')² dx - 2 Σᵢ wᵢ ln ψ(xᵢ) - Σⱼ rⱼ ln ∫_{Bⱼ} ψ² dx

subject to ∫ ψ² dx = 1, where wᵢ counts the point observations at xᵢ and rⱼ the observations in interval Bⱼ. Between consecutive endpoints, ψ'' = k²ψ with k² = κ²(1 - rⱼ/(λPⱼ)) inside interval j (Pⱼ its fitted probability) and k² = κ² outside every interval. Inside a well-populated interval k² < 0 and ψ is a trigonometric arc, so the density is a smooth bump across the interval. The problem is convex in the density, and the fit is found by Newton's method on the nodal densities at O(m) cost per step for m distinct endpoints, provided each interval contains few interior points.

The fields d.x (nodes), d.ψ (normalized amplitudes there), d.k2 (the realized k² on each cell between nodes), and d.kL, d.kR (the tails' decay rates) describe the fitted curve. d.κ is the smoothing scale. d.w counts only the point observations, so the total count is nobs(d).

As for DensityEstimate, passing a SolveStats as the stats keyword records the Newton-step and backtracking counts of the solve; it does not change the fit.

PenalizedDensity.SolveStats — Type
SolveStats()

Mutable collector for diagnostics of the fixed-scale Newton solve. Pass one as the stats keyword to DensityEstimate or IntervalDensityEstimate to record:

  • iterations: the number of Newton steps taken (each a Hessian factorization that updated the amplitudes);
  • backtracks: the total number of Armijo step-halvings across those steps;
  • reason: why the iteration stopped:
    • :tolerance, the Newton correction fell to eps(T)^(3/4) relative to ψ (normal convergence);
    • :floor, the correction stopped decreasing before reaching that tolerance, limited by the roundoff floor of a near-singular Hessian;
    • :steplength, backtracking found no positive step moving ψ by more than rounding, so ψ is stationary to the available precision;
    • :none, left unset (the solve threw before terminating);
  • final_step: the last Newton correction magnitude, maxᵢ |Δᵢ|/ψᵢ; at or below the tolerance on a :tolerance exit, above it on a :floor exit;
  • final_alpha: the damping α of the last accepted step, 1 when it was undamped.

Use a new collector for each fit. Recording does not change the solve.

source
PenalizedDensity.WindowRule — Type
WindowRule(; gamma=0.75, k=0.11, z=4, nmin=30)

Settings of the window candidate of Adaptive(; window), whose default is WindowRule().

The candidate is the scale κ(x) = c·(1/ĥ(x))^gamma (a WindowScale), where ĥ(x) is the smallest half-width at which a window centered on x holds at least nmin observations and its first or second moment departs from that of a uniform spread by at least z standard deviations; c is chosen by the same cross-validation score as the other candidates. The window candidate is chosen only if its score plus the penalty k·gamma/√N, for N observations, is below the score of the scale the power family would return.

gamma and z must be positive, k nonnegative, and nmin at least 2.

Extended help

The penalty offsets the optimism of a held-out score whose scale was estimated from the held-out point itself; see the extended help of select_kappa.

source
PenalizedDensity.WindowScale — Type
WindowScale(c, γ, x, logb)

A spatially varying smoothing scale

κ(t) = c · exp(γ · s(t)),

where s interpolates the values logb linearly on the sorted grid x and is held constant beyond it. select_kappa(x, Adaptive()) constructs one with s = ln(1/ĥ) from the window rule (see WindowRule), shifted to mean zero over the sample, so that c is the scale at a point of typical window size. Like AdaptiveScale, the result is callable and can be passed to DensityEstimate as the smoothing scale.

Extended help

The scale is floored at 1e-6 c.

PenalizedDensity.action — Method
action(d::AbstractDensityEstimate) -> S

Classical action S[ψ_cl] (Eq. 10) of the fitted density,

S = N - λ - Σᵢ wᵢ ln Q̂(xᵢ) - Σⱼ rⱼ ln Pⱼ,

where wᵢ counts the point observations at xᵢ, and, for an IntervalDensityEstimate, rⱼ counts the observations in interval Bⱼ and Pⱼ = ∫_Bⱼ Q̂ dx is the fitted probability of Bⱼ; N = Σ wᵢ + Σ rⱼ. Used by select_kappa_sensitivity.

Extended help

At the fit the roughness term ∫ (λ/κ²)(ψ')² dx equals N - λ, which is what reduces the action to the form above.

source
PenalizedDensity.action_sensitivity — Method
action_sensitivity(x, κ; rtol=cbrt(eps(T))) -> s::T

The minimum-sensitivity score s = |dS/d ln κ| of the scale κ for data x, where S is the classical action of the unbounded fit DensityEstimate(x, κ; rtol). select_kappa_sensitivity returns the κ minimizing it; evaluating it over a range of κ shows how sharply that minimum is defined. The derivative is computed analytically. Like select_kappa_sensitivity, it takes point data only.

PenalizedDensity.cdf — Method
cdf(d::AbstractDensityEstimate, x)

Cumulative distribution function of the fitted density, F(x) = ∫_a^x Q̂(t) dt with Q̂ = ψ² and a the fit's left support endpoint (-Inf unless the fit was given a finite support). F is nondecreasing, with F(x) == 0 for x ≤ a and F(x) == 1 for x ≥ b, where b is the right support endpoint.

See quantile for the inverse.

Examples

julia> d = DensityEstimate([0.0], 0.5);   # a Laplace density centered at 0

julia> cdf(d, 0.0)
0.5

julia> quantile(d, 0.5)
0.0

Extended help

F is evaluated without adaptive quadrature. For a DensityEstimate, the tails (or, on a finite support, the two boundary segments) are pure exponentials or cosh arcs; on each inter-node interval ψ'' = κ²ψ makes ψ'² - κ²ψ² constant, which yields the exact antiderivative of the hyperbolic interpolant. An IntervalDensityEstimate uses the same antiderivative on its hyperbolic cells and 16-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature on its trigonometric ones, where ψ² is a trigonometric polynomial of low enough frequency that the rule is exact to roundoff. F(a) == 0 and F(b) == 1 hold exactly because a global rescaling absorbs the few ulps of normalization roundoff in the fitted amplitudes.

source
PenalizedDensity.chisq — Method
chisq(d::DensityEstimate, Q) -> χ²

Goodness-of-fit statistic between a trial density Q and the data underlying the fit d, the robust field-theoretic analogue of Pearson's χ² (Eqs. 13–14 of the paper):

χ² = 4 Σᵢ wᵢ (√Q(xᵢ) / ψ_cl(xᵢ) - 1)²,

summed over the data nodes xᵢ with multiplicities wᵢ, where ψ_cl = √(d(·)) is the fitted amplitude. Q is any callable returning density values; it should be a normalized density (∫Q dx = 1). chisq(d, d) == 0. Small χ² means Q is close to the data in squared Hellinger distance. For its significance, build the reference law once with ChisqReference and pass the statistic to pvalue.

Defined for point data only; there is no form for an IntervalDensityEstimate.

source
PenalizedDensity.chisq_ccdf — Method
chisq_ccdf(ref::ChisqReference, z; method=:exact, rtol, atol) -> P(χ² ≥ z)

Upper-tail (survival) probability of the reference χ² distribution at z. Evaluated at an observed statistic it is a diagnostic significance; see pvalue, and ChisqReference for the sense in which it is and is not a p-value.

method=:exact (default) uses the finite-N generalized-χ² law of ref via Imhof inversion. method=:largeN uses the inverse-Gaussian (Wald) shape of the large-N limit (Eq. 26), parameterized by the exact mean mean(ref); it is in closed form and far cheaper per call. rtol and atol (default sqrt(eps(T))) are the tolerances of the :exact quadrature, and a quadrature that does not reach them throws a ConvergenceError. The law is supported on z > 0: z ≤ 0 gives 1, z = Inf gives 0, and NaN throws a DomainError.

source
PenalizedDensity.chisq_pdf — Method
chisq_pdf(ref::ChisqReference, z; method=:exact, rtol, atol) -> P(z)

Density of the reference χ² distribution at z; it is 0 for z ≤ 0 and at z = Inf, and NaN throws a DomainError. method=:exact (default) is the finite-N generalized-χ² law of ref. method=:largeN is the inverse-Gaussian (Wald) density of the large-N limit (Eq. 26), parameterized by the exact mean mean(ref). rtol and atol are as in chisq_ccdf, including the ConvergenceError.

Extended help

The method=:largeN density is

P(z) = ⟨χ²⟩ / √(2π z³) · exp[⟨χ²⟩ - z/2 - ⟨χ²⟩²/(2z)],

with ⟨χ²⟩ = mean(ref) the exact mean.

source
PenalizedDensity.entropy — Method
entropy(d::AbstractDensityEstimate, xeval::AbstractVector) -> Ĥ

Held-out plug-in estimate of the differential entropy of the fitted density d, scored at points xeval that did not enter the fit d:

Ĥ = -(1/M) Σⱼ ln Q̂(xevalⱼ),   M = length(xeval),

with ln Q̂ evaluated by logdensity, so it stays finite far into an unbounded tail where Q̂ itself underflows. A point outside a finite support has ln Q̂ = -Inf and makes Ĥ = Inf.

Unlike the one-argument entropy(d), which evaluates ln Q̂ at the fit's own nodes, this evaluates it at independent points and so does not reward collapsing the density onto the fit sample. It is the entropy term of negentropy(d, xeval).

source
PenalizedDensity.entropy — Method
entropy(d::AbstractDensityEstimate) -> Ĥ

Plug-in estimate of the differential entropy H(Q) = -∫ Q ln Q dx, computed from the fitted density Q̂,

Ĥ = -(1/N) [Σᵢ wᵢ ln Q̂(xᵢ) + Σⱼ rⱼ ⟨ln Q̂⟩_Bⱼ],   ⟨ln Q̂⟩_B = ∫_B Q̂ ln Q̂ dx / ∫_B Q̂ dx,

where wᵢ counts the point observations at xᵢ, and, for an IntervalDensityEstimate, rⱼ counts the observations in interval Bⱼ; N = Σ wᵢ + Σ rⱼ. Evaluating ln Q̂ at the data rather than integrating it over the whole line is consistent (Ĥ → H(Q) as the sample grows), but it is biased at small N. See negentropy.

Extended help

As an example of the small-N bias, for the one-point fit (a Laplace density with rate 2κ), Ĥ = -ln κ against the fit's exact entropy H(Q̂) = 1 - ln κ.

An interval observation contributes the fit's mean of ln Q̂ over its interval, the expected value of the ln Q̂(xᵢ) its unrecorded value would have contributed. As an interval shrinks to a point this becomes the point term, so an interval fit to point observations only has the same Ĥ as DensityEstimate(x, κ). A censored observation is handled the same way; on an unbounded tail ⟨ln Q̂⟩ = ln Q̂(x_edge) - 1 exactly.

source
PenalizedDensity.gaussianize — Method
gaussianize(d::AbstractDensityEstimate, x)

Gaussianizing transport of the fitted density: y = Φ⁻¹(F(x)), where F =cdf(d, ·) and Φ is the standard normal CDF. If X is distributed with density d, then gaussianize(d, X) is distributed N(0, 1); the map is strictly increasing wherever the density is positive. NaN throws a DomainError.

A finite support (see DensityEstimate) and a per-interval κ are both supported. On a finite support the walls map to infinity: gaussianize(d, x) == -Inf for x at or below a finite left endpoint and +Inf at or above a finite right endpoint (there cdf(d, x) is exactly 0 or 1), and ungaussianize maps such a y back to the wall.

See ungaussianize for the inverse and gaussianize_logjacobian for the map together with its log-Jacobian.

Examples

julia> d = DensityEstimate([0.0], 0.5);   # a Laplace density centered at 0

PenalizedDensity.gaussianize_logjacobian — Method
gaussianize_logjacobian(d::AbstractDensityEstimate, x) -> (; y, logjac)

The Gaussianizing map y =gaussianize(d, x) together with the log-Jacobian of the transport,

logjac = ln Q̂(x) - ln φ(y),

where Q̂ is the fitted density and φ the standard normal density. Thus logjac = ln(dy/dx), the change-of-variables term that makes ln φ(y) + logjac equal ln Q̂(x). Past a finite support wall the density is zero and logjac == -Inf (the map is constant there).

Broadcasting, r = gaussianize_logjacobian.(d, xs), returns an array of (; y, logjac) pairs; first.(r) and last.(r) unpack it. The plain derivative dy/dx is exp(logjac).

Extended help

In an unbounded tail the difference ln Q̂(x) - ln φ(y) is evaluated in a cancellation-free closed form (both terms grow like y²/2), so logjac stays accurate arbitrarily far out. Inside a boundary segment of a finite support the density is finite, so logjac is finite and grows toward the wall as the map stretches it to infinity.

PenalizedDensity.select_kappa — Method
select_kappa(x, adaptive::Adaptive; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(T)),
             resolution=nothing) -> κ
select_kappa(lower, upper, adaptive::Adaptive; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(T))) -> κ

Choose a spatially varying smoothing scale by Kullback–Leibler cross-validation, and return it ready to pass to DensityEstimate (or, for interval data, IntervalDensityEstimate). κ is a T, an AdaptiveScale{T} or a WindowScale{T}, with T the data's floating-point type.

Three kinds of scale compete on the same score: the constant scale, returned as a number (so the fit takes the constant-κ path and its goodness-of-fit machinery stays available); the power family c·(p̂/ḡ)^α over a pilot density p̂, returned as an AdaptiveScale; and the window rule, returned as a WindowScale. The power family refines the scale where the density is high; the window rule refines it where a small window around x already shows a slope or curvature, and so resolves narrow features whatever their height. The window candidate is returned when its score plus a penalty (see WindowRule) is below that of the power family's choice. Within the power family, an exponent α is returned when it beats the constant scale by more than nse standard errors of the score. The settings alphas, window, nse and pilot are those of adaptive (see Adaptive). Selection costs a small multiple of one constant-scale select_kappa call per exponent plus one for the window candidate; a shorter alphas is faster but searches fewer exponents.

support, rtol and resolution work as for the constant-scale select_kappa. There is no holdout keyword: every score, including the pilot's, uses holdout = :location. support is fixed across every candidate α/c, and both the pilot density and every candidate are fitted and cross-validated on it. With resolution = δ > 0 the scale is selected for IntervalDensityEstimate(x, κ; resolution=δ).

To choose support as well, call select_support first, pass its support here, and fit DensityEstimate(x, κ; support) with the scale this returns.

Examples

julia> x = -log.(1 .- (0.5:999.5) ./ 1000);   # exponential: a jump at the left edge

julia> κ = select_kappa(x, Adaptive());      # the window rule wins here

julia> κ isa WindowScale
true

julia> d = DensityEstimate(x, κ);

julia> extrema(d.κ)[2] / extrema(d.κ)[1] > 10    # far finer at the edge than in the tail
true

julia> select_kappa(x, Adaptive(window=nothing)).α   # the power family alone
0.625

julia> select_kappa(range(0, 1; length=1000), Adaptive(window=nothing)) isa Real   # nothing to buy
true

Extended help

A single scale must trade resolution in the bulk against noise in the tails. Letting κ follow the density relaxes that trade-off, and helps most where a constant scale is limited by the density's own irregularity rather than by noise: a divergent or discontinuous edge, a kink, heavy tails, or narrow features beside broad ones. On smooth densities the gain is small, and the selector returns a plain number, the constant scale, when it scores best; with nse > 0 the power family must also beat it by that many standard errors.

The rule is a plug-in: fit a pilot density p̂ at the constant scale pilot(x) (by default select_kappa), then consider the family

κ(x; c, α) = c · (p̂(x) / ḡ)^α,     ḡ = geometric mean of p̂ over the sample

(an AdaptiveScale). For each exponent α in alphas, c is chosen by golden-section search on the leave-one-out score KLCV(κ) = -(1/N) Σᵢ wᵢ ln Q̂₋ᵢ(xᵢ) generalized to a varying scale. This is the criterion select_kappa minimizes, and it is likewise evaluated in closed form in O(N), with no refitting. The constant scale competes as the α = 0 member of the same family and on the same score.

The exponent with the smallest score is the adaptive candidate; it is then compared with the constant scale by a one-standard-error rule. Each observation xⱼ contributes a gain dⱼ = ln Q̂₋ⱼ(xⱼ; adaptive) - ln Q̂₋ⱼ(xⱼ; constant), where each observation takes the leave-one-out density of the node it was merged into under that scale (the two scales can merge the sample into different nodes). The mean of dⱼ is the difference of the two scores, gain = KLCV(constant) - KLCV(adaptive), and se = std(d)/√N is its standard error. The adaptive scale is returned only if gain > nse · se. If se == 0 (including N = 1), it is returned iff gain > 0. A candidate whose score is not finite (an unresolvable fit) never wins, and an exponent with no resolvable c near the previous exponent's is skipped; if the constant scale is the unresolvable one, the best finite adaptive candidate is returned regardless of nse. The rule applies only to this final choice, not among the exponents.

With window = WindowRule(; gamma, k, z, nmin), the window half-width ĥ(x) is the smallest on a logarithmic scan (24 steps per decade, starting at the distance to the nmin-th nearest observation) at which the window |t - x| ≤ ĥ holds at least nmin observations and either of its statistics

|Σu| / √(N/3)      or      |Σu² - N/3| / √(4N/45),      u = (t - x)/ĥ,

over its N observations t reaches z. Each is a moment's departure from its value for points spread uniformly over the window, in units of its standard deviation under that spread: a slope and a curvature test of "no structure within the window". ĥ is computed on a grid of 200 evenly spaced points over the sample's range plus 201 sample quantiles (prefix sums make each window cost two binary searches) and interpolated linearly between them. The candidate is κ(x) = c·exp(gamma·s(x)) with s = ln(1/ĥ) shifted to mean zero over the sample, c chosen by the same golden-section search on KLCV as for the power family, starting from the pilot scale. It is returned if

KLCV(window) + k·gamma/√N  <  KLCV(power),

where KLCV(power) is the score of the scale the power family would return (the constant scale or an AdaptiveScale, chosen with nse as above); a window candidate with no resolvable c never wins. The penalty accounts for ĥ depending on the held-out point itself: KLCV treats the shape as fixed, and so rates the window candidate too well, by an amount that grows with gamma and shrinks like 1/√N. The default k = 0.11 matches the optimism measured by exact leave-one-out refits (with ĥ re-estimated without the held-out point) on smooth and bimodal densities at N = 1000 and 4000. The threshold z = 4 keeps the scan above the noise of the window statistics, which have unit standard deviation in any window without structure. gamma is fixed rather than selected per sample, because a per-sample choice of gamma is dominated by sampling noise in the score.

For rounded or interval data, the observations recorded in an interval are placed at its quantile midpoints, lower + (upper - lower)·(j - ½)/m for the m observations of that interval, and the window statistics are computed from these locations; exact observations stay where they are, and observations with an infinite bound (censored) enter the score but not the shape. Within a window this gives the sums of u and u² expected if each observation were spread uniformly over its interval, while the counts in different intervals keep their sampling noise. The pilot is an interval fit, every candidate is scored by the held-out log-probabilities of the observations' intervals (the interval-likelihood KLCV scores), and N counts all observations.

pilot is a scale-selection method, and is called on the sample alone with no notion of support; the pilot density it scales is what is fitted on support. So a selector with no notion of a boundary, like select_kappa_sensitivity, remains usable as pilot on a bounded domain. Because support is fixed throughout the α search, passing the support from select_support runs the search on that domain, so the chosen α reflects the boundary rather than being taken from an unbounded selection.

PenalizedDensity.select_kappa — Method
select_kappa(x; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(T)), resolution=nothing,
             holdout=:location, kappas=nothing) -> κ::T
select_kappa(lower, upper; support=(-Inf, Inf), rtol=cbrt(eps(T)), holdout=:location,
             kappas=nothing) -> κ::T

Choose a constant smoothing scale for DensityEstimate(x, κ; support, rtol) (or, for interval data, IntervalDensityEstimate) by Kullback–Leibler (likelihood) cross-validation: return the κ minimizing the mean negative leave-one-out log-likelihood

KLCV(κ) = -(1/N) Σᵢ wᵢ ln Q̂_{κ,-i}(xᵢ),

where Q̂_{κ,-i} is the density fitted with the i-th point left out. κ has the data's floating-point type T. For a spatially varying scale, see select_kappa(x, ::Adaptive); to choose support as well, see select_support.

support, rtol and resolution describe the model and should match the later fit:

  • support = (a, b) (default (-Inf, Inf)) fits and cross-validates on a finite domain, as DensityEstimate's support does. It is held fixed across every candidate κ. Data outside [a, b], or a ≥ b, throws a DomainError.
  • rtol is the node-merging tolerance, as a fraction of the local smoothing length.
  • Cross-validation of the point likelihood assumes the data are draws from a continuous density. If more than 1% of the observations repeat another value, the data are probably rounded, and an ArgumentError explains the options unless resolution is given. resolution = δ > 0 declares that each x[i] was rounded to a lattice of spacing δ; the scale is then chosen for IntervalDensityEstimate(x, κ; resolution=δ), and each observation contributes the held-out log-probability of its rounding interval. resolution = 0 declares the values exact points, fitted by DensityEstimate.

The second form takes the bounds of interval observations directly, for IntervalDensityEstimate(lower, upper, κ), including censored values and a mix of points and intervals.

holdout chooses what each held-out term for a point observation leaves out: :location (the default) all observations at the held-out value, :observation one of them. The two agree when every value is distinct. On tied data leaving out a single copy rewards density spikes at the repeated values. Interval observations are always left out one at a time.

kappas is the grid that brackets the search. By default (kappas = nothing) it is a geometric range scaled to the data's extent, extended past either end when the minimum falls there. A grid passed as kappas must be sorted and positive, with at least three values, and is searched as given: a minimum on its first or last value throws a ConvergenceError.

klcv evaluates the score at a given κ. See the "Comparing selection criteria" section of the manual for the alternative criteria select_kappa_lscv and select_kappa_sensitivity.

Extended help

KLCV estimates, up to a κ-independent constant, the Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(Q ‖ Q̂_κ); minimizing it is maximum-likelihood cross-validation. It is the criterion native to the estimator, whose action -Σ ln Q̂(xᵢ) is itself the (in-sample) log-likelihood. To leading order it selects the same error-optimal scale as least-squares cross-validation (select_kappa_lscv), and it is cheaper, because the ∫Q̂² roughness term is not needed. For interval data it estimates the divergence between the true and fitted probabilities of the recorded intervals, which is all such data determine.

Each leave-one-out density Q̂_{-i}(xᵢ) comes from a one-step leave-one-out expansion of the fit in the dropped point's weight: a Newton step whose length is set so the deleted node's own stationarity condition holds exactly. This tracks a full refit closely and, unlike a linear step, responds to over-fitting; no per-point refitting is needed, and the score costs O(N). For an interval observation, the refit is approximated by minimizing over the interval's own nodal values with its term in the likelihood exact and the rest of the problem replaced by its quadratic model, which stays accurate when the deletion empties the interval. The score is minimized by a golden-section search over ln κ, bracketed by the grid kappas.

For interval data the default kappas grid never extends past the κ at which κ times the widest interval reaches 4096, beyond which the fit cannot be computed accurately; a minimum there is returned as the selected scale, since the fit is then at its histogram limit. When both ends of support are finite, the default grid never extends below κ = 0.1/(hi - lo), where the fit is already close to the uniform density on the support; a minimum there is returned as the selected scale.

PenalizedDensity.select_kappa_lscv — Method
select_kappa_lscv(x; kappas=nothing, rtol=cbrt(eps(T)), support=(-Inf, Inf),
                  resolution=nothing, holdout=:location) -> κ

Choose the smoothing scale by least-squares cross-validation: return the κ minimizing lscv(x, κ),

LSCV(κ) = ∫ Q̂_κ(x)² dx - (2/N) Σᵢ Q̂_{κ,-i}(xᵢ),

an unbiased estimate, up to the κ-independent ∫Q², of the integrated squared error ∫(Q̂_κ - Q)², where Q̂_{κ,-i} is the density fitted with the i-th point left out. Its minimizer therefore targets minimum mean integrated squared error (MISE).

support, kappas, rtol and holdout mean what they do for select_kappa. resolution may be nothing (check for ties) or 0 (accept the values as exact points); a positive resolution throws an ArgumentError. This criterion has no interval form; for rounded data use select_kappa with resolution set to the rounding increment.

Extended help

LSCV generally selects a coarser scale than select_kappa_sensitivity (minimum sensitivity), which resolves information rather than squared error and tends to over-resolve smooth densities. There is no interval form because, when only the interval of each observation is known, the cross term has no unbiased estimate.

Both terms are evaluated analytically in O(N): ∫Q̂² in closed form over the exponential segments, and each leave-one-out density Q̂_{-i}(xᵢ) from the same leave-one-out expansion select_kappa uses, so no per-point refitting is needed. The score is minimized by a golden-section search over ln κ, bracketed by the grid kappas.

PenalizedDensity.select_kappa_sensitivity — Method
select_kappa_sensitivity(x; kappas=nothing, rtol=cbrt(eps(T))) -> κ

Choose the smoothing scale by the principle of minimum sensitivity: return the κ at which the classical action action S is least sensitive to the scale, i.e. the κ minimizing action_sensitivity(x, κ) = |dS/d ln κ| (Fig. 1 of the paper). kappas is the grid that brackets the search, as for select_kappa.

This criterion resolves information and over-resolves smooth densities; to target estimation error, use select_kappa (or select_kappa_lscv). For rounded data, pass the rounding increment to select_kappa as resolution.

This selector takes no support keyword: it always fits (and returns a scale for) the unbounded problem. It also has no form for interval data.

Extended help

There is no support keyword because the entropy asymptotics behind minimum sensitivity are derived for the unbounded line and do not generalize to a finite domain.

The derivative dS/d ln κ is evaluated analytically and minimized over κ by a golden-section search, bracketed by the grid kappas. The result is a convention rather than a unique optimum: S has no exact stationary point in κ, so the flattest point depends on measuring sensitivity in ln κ.

PenalizedDensity.select_support — Method
select_support(x; rtol=cbrt(eps(T)), resolution=nothing, kappas=nothing)
    -> (; κ, support)

Choose a domain support = (a, b), either side possibly infinite, jointly with the smoothing scale κ, by the Kullback–Leibler cross-validation score that select_kappa minimizes. κ has the data's floating-point type. Pass the result to DensityEstimate:

r = select_support(x)
d = DensityEstimate(x, r.κ; support = r.support)

A boundary is imposed on a side only when it wins that cross-validation; otherwise that side stays ±Inf. A finite boundary is always placed outward of the extreme data point on its side, and never closer to it than five times the mean spacing of the data near that edge. When neither side wins, the support is (-Inf, Inf) and the returned κ equals select_kappa(x; rtol, kappas) exactly.

kappas and rtol mean what they do for select_kappa: the grid bracketing the κ search (by default extended as needed), and the node-merging tolerance (a fraction of the local smoothing length).

More than 1% repeated values throw an ArgumentError, as for select_kappa, unless resolution = 0 is given, which treats the values as exact points. select_support has no form for rounded (interval) data, so resolution > 0 throws an ArgumentError.

Examples

julia> x = -log.(1 .- (0.5:499.5) ./ 500);   # exponential draw: a jump edge at the left

julia> r = select_support(x);

julia> r.support[1] <= minimum(x) && r.support[2] == Inf   # never inward of the data
true

julia> d = DensityEstimate(x, r.κ; support = r.support);

julia> d.lo == r.support[1] && d.hi == r.support[2]
true

Extended help

The sides are searched in sequence: the left boundary first (with the right side unbounded), then the right boundary given the left side's result. On each side the unbounded (±Inf) candidate competes, and the side gets a finite boundary only if the best finite candidate's KLCV beats the unbounded score by more than a small margin, which screens out golden-section and floating-point noise. A wall placed too far past the data can raise the KLCV score: the flat field near the wall puts mass into an empty margin where a decaying tail would not.

A finite candidate on one side is a gap Δ > 0, the distance outward from the extreme data point on that side (a = x₁ - Δ on the left, b = x_N + Δ on the right), searched by golden-section on ln Δ over a bracket of [5, 100] times the mean spacing of the ten data points nearest that edge (extensible outward, never inward). The lower end is a hard floor: closer than a few edge spacings, a natural boundary reflects the nearest interior points back onto the extreme point and inflates its leave-one-out likelihood on any sample, whether or not the density has an edge. This is a property of the reflecting boundary condition, also seen in brute-force leave-one-out refits, so gaps below the floor are excluded. κ is re-selected at every gap candidate, because the two are coupled at a hard edge (the optimal κ can fall to a fraction of its unbounded value once a wall is added). Candidates are searched from the widest gap to the narrowest, and since κ* moves continuously with the gap, each candidate's κ search is confined to a narrow window about the previous candidate's optimum. The κ searched at each finite-boundary candidate stays within the range of the grid the unbounded search used.

The full constant-scale search of select_kappa runs at two points only: once at the start, to seed the unbounded arm's competing score and the first (and widest) gap candidate's κ warm start; and once at the end, to refine κ at the winning support over the full kappas grid (the inner searches above use a narrower window). When neither side wins, no refinement is made and the returned κ is the first search's result.

ungaussianize(d::AbstractDensityEstimate, y)

Inverse of gaussianize: x = F⁻¹(Φ(y)) with F⁻¹ =quantile(d, ·), so that ungaussianize(d, gaussianize(d, x)) ≈ x. If Y ~ N(0, 1), then ungaussianize(d, Y) is distributed with density d, so mapping standard normal variates through this function samples from the fit. NaN throws a DomainError.

Extended help

To avoid cancellation, for y > 0 the complement 1 - Φ(y) is computed directly rather than Φ(y), and deep tails run in log space through the closed-form exponential-tail inverse, so large |y| maps to the corresponding finite x rather than saturating to ±Inf. ungaussianize(d, ±Inf) == ±Inf.

Statistics.quantile — Method
quantile(d::AbstractDensityEstimate, q)

Quantile function of the fitted density, the inverse of cdf: cdf(d, quantile(d, q)) ≈ q for q ∈ [0, 1], with quantile(d, 0) == a and quantile(d, 1) == b (the fit's support endpoints, -Inf/Inf unless the fit was given a finite support); q outside [0, 1] (including NaN) throws a DomainError. Extends Statistics.quantile.

Extended help

In an unbounded tail the inversion is in closed form; on interior intervals, and on a finite boundary segment (transcendental there), a Newton iteration on the closed-form CDF, bracketed by the enclosing nodes or by the wall and the outermost node, converges to floating-point accuracy. The right side is solved through 1 - q, so upper quantiles lose no more precision than q itself carries.

StatsAPI.pvalue — Method
pvalue(ref::ChisqReference, χ²; method=:exact, rtol, atol) -> p

Significance of an observed goodness-of-fit statistic χ² =chisq(d, Q) for a trial density Q, where ref =ChisqReference(d): the probability that the reference χ² distribution exceeds it, chisq_ccdf(ref, χ²; kwargs...). Examples
